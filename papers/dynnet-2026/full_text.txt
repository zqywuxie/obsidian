Abstract 
Single-cell RNA sequencing has revolutionized our ability to dissect cellular heterogeneity and study cell 
fate mechanisms, yet inferring stochastic dynamics from static snapshots remains a fundamental 
challenge. Current approaches face a critical trade-off: mechanistic models impose rigid assumptions 
limiting biological realism, while data-driven methods sacrifice interpretability for deeper mechanistic 
explorations. Here, we present DynNet, a deep learning method that integrates Neural ODEs with 
biophysical models and prior knowledge of gene expression dynamics. DynNet learns the stochastic 
dynamics of gene regulatory systems for cell fate decisions. Benchmarking on synthetic data shows 
DynNet's ability to infer stable cell states, reconstruct dynamical trajectories, and characterize multi-
stable cell fate transitions. Using hepatocyte differentiation data, DynNet demonstrates its capability to 
infer developmental trajectory and the underlying cell fate landscape, revealing the stability and transition 
probabilities among distinct cell states. Applied to Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) data, 
DynNet further captures critical gene regulations and transition paths during EMT.

Introduction 
Stochastic dynamical transitions are fundamental in biological, physical, and chemical systems1–5 and are 
especially crucial for understanding cell fate determinations6,7. The existence of multiple stable steady 
states allows cells to transition between distinct fates in response to external or internal cues. This 
mechanism is particularly important in stem cell differentiation8, immune cell activation9, and cancer cell 
phenotypic switching10,11. 
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has enabled the study of cellular functions12,13, 
including lineage decision, gene regulatory networks, and disease mechanisms at cellular resolution14–16. 
However, scRNA-seq involves the destruction of cells to measure their molecular state, making it 
impossible to directly track the specific molecular trajectories of individual cells over time17. This 
limitation in capturing temporal dynamics has spurred the development of computational approaches to 
reconstruct the dynamics of system behaviours. 
Various computational methods have been developed, including Markov-model-based approaches and 
deep learning methods, to reconstruct cell fate trajectories and model gene expression dynamics18–22. 
These methods are now widely used to reconstruct developmental processes22–26 and disease 
progression27,28. Markov-model-based methods focus on reconstructing cell state trajectories and ordering 
cells in pseudo-time. Nevertheless, these types of methods lack a quantitative, interpretable dynamic 
model explicitly linked to underlying gene regulatory mechanisms18,19,29. Neural ordinary equations 
(Neural ODEs) use neural networks and differential equations, allowing reconstruction of gene expression 
dynamics continuously from data20,21,30–32. However, classic deterministic Neural ODEs fail to capture the 
multi-stable dynamics as only a definite direction of dynamics is allowed at any given point20,33–35. 
Moreover, it is difficult for Neural ODE frameworks to include prior knowledge, for example, specific 
gene regulatory interactions, and often the dynamics on low-dimensional latent spaces is derived from the 
high-dimensional gene expression data, limiting the direct interpretability of the inferred dynamics at the

level of individual genes. RNA velocity-based methods like scVelo are effective for inferring cellular 
dynamics along trajectories by capturing the rates of spliced and unspliced transcripts16,24,31,36. Multi-
omics data, such as metabolic markers or lineage information, might be used to estimate unspliced RNA 
sequences37. In the absence of additional measurements, inferring interpretable dynamics involving 
underlying gene regulatory mechanisms solely from scRNA-seq data remains a challenge. As gene 
regulatory network (GRN) models provide interpretable frameworks for understanding transition 
dynamics between different stable states38–42, it’s important to bridge mechanistic modelling and data 
driven approaches43–47. In addition, energy landscape methods provide effective ways to characterize 
stability, transitions, and stochastic dynamics for cell fate decision processes in gene regulatory 
networks41,44,48. 
Here we present DynNet (Stochastic Dynamics Inference Network) to infer the latent gene regulatory 
dynamics. Crucially, DynNet leverages scRNA-seq data collected at multiple, discrete, and known time 
points, utilizing the temporal information as constraints to model the underlying continuous process. Our 
core idea is to employ stochastic differential equations (SDEs), the natural mathematical framework for 
stochastic simulation, to mechanistically decouple the dynamics into a deterministic gene regulatory drift 
term and a diffusion term representing stochastic fluctuations, allowing for interpretable, single-cell level 
inference. In this approach, the model jointly optimises biophysical parameters and network weights by 
incorporating prior regulatory knowledge via evidence-tiered constraints. To capture the inherent 
stochasticity of gene expression dynamics, DynNet employs a neural network to estimate the noise 
variance dynamically, allowing for a more accurate description of single-cell data. DynNet also integrates 
landscape reconstruction within its framework to infer the underlying dynamical structure of gene 
regulatory systems. By embedding energy landscape analysis directly into its modelling process, DynNet 
provides a tool to identify transition driver genes and important regulations, through perturbation analysis.  
To evaluate DynNet, we apply it to representative synthetic datasets, including mutually inhibited self-
activation (MISA) model and cell differentiation gene network. DynNet can efficiently reconstruct steady

The Mutual Inhibition and Self-Activation (MISA) model54,55 is a classical gene regulatory motif in which 
two genes mutually inhibit each other while self-activating themselves (Figure 2A and Figure S5). The 
system's multi-stability property makes it a mathematically tractable benchmark for dynamic inference 
methods. We generated synthetic data comprising five temporal snapshots (2000 samples per time point), 
capturing transitions from initial states to equilibrium. Phase space analysis of the simulated data revealed 
three stable states: an X1-dominant differentiated state (S1), an X2-dominant differentiated state (S2), and 
a balanced pluripotent state (S3) with moderate expression levels (Figure 2B). To evaluate DynNet's 
performance in multi-stable systems, we established a comprehensive analysis framework assessing three 
key aspects: stable state localization accuracy, single-cell fate prediction reliability, and global dynamic 
reconstruction capability. This framework allows for a comprehensive verification of the model's capacity 
to resolve multi-stable configurations, track single-cell developmental paths, and recover dynamical 
landscapes. 
To assess the ability of DynNet to recover and quantify the properties of the system's stable states, we 
compared its predictions against the ground truth simulation data of MISA. DynNet correctly identified 
the presence of three stable states at the last time point. We then quantitatively compared DynNet's 
estimated positions and variances of these stable states to those derived from ground truth data. This 
comparison indicates that DynNet accurately estimates stable state centers, showing close agreement with 
observed data (Figure 2C). Furthermore, the covariance regions, reflecting stable state variability, also 
show alignment with the simulation data. Beyond position and covariance, DynNet's estimates of the 
marginal distributions for variables X1 and X2 also closely match those observed in the ground truth data 
(Figure 2D and Figure S6). The reconstructed energy landscape shows agreement with the overall 
distribution and shape of the data (Figure 2E). Consistent performance in reconstructing stable state 
properties was also observed when applying DynNet to bistable data (Figure S7 and S8). Collectively, 
these findings demonstrate DynNet's capacity to accurately identify and quantify key stable state 
properties, including their number, locations, associated uncertainties and marginal distributions. This

demonstrated capacity is crucial for precisely defining distinct cellular states and confirms DynNet's 
reliability in accurately predicting and comprehensively characterizing the stable states of multi-stable 
systems.  
To validate single-cell fate predictability, we assessed DynNet's performance by formulating stable state 
prediction as a classification task based on decision boundaries. As a quantitative measure of prediction 
reliability, decision boundaries delineate the regions of phase space leading to different stable states (see 
Supplementary Information Section D). The grid-structured dataset produced by the MISA model 
delineates the ground truth decision boundaries, serving as a benchmark for our comparative analysis 
(Figure 2F left). We then applied the trained DynNet model to predict the stable state for each point in the 
grid-structured dataset and derived its corresponding decision boundaries (Figure 2F right). DynNet's 
predicted decision boundaries demonstrate consistency with those obtained from the simulation data, 
accurately dividing the phase space into the three stable state basins of attraction. This agreement further 
demonstrates the effectiveness of DynNet in delineating the region of attraction in phase space. We also 
compare the decision boundaries between DynNet and Neural ODE method as benchmark method (see 
Supplementary Information Section C). The decision boundaries predicted by DynNet are more accurate 
than those predicted by the classic Neural ODE method. These results demonstrate that for some cells 
with an undetermined fate, DynNet can effectively predict their future fate (Figure S9 and S10).  
To evaluate DynNet's ability to reconstruct the system's global dynamic behaviour, we first assessed its 
performance in reconstructing the overall velocity field. We computed the velocity field of the simulated 
data as a benchmark (Figure 2H and Figure S11). The velocity field estimated from DynNet not only 
captured key features such as decision boundaries and stable states but also demonstrated better 
performance in approximating the true dynamics compared to the Neural ODE method across datasets 
with varying numbers of time points (2, 3, and 5) (Figure 2G and Figure S11). Unlike the traditional 
Neural ODE, which struggled to accurately reconstruct dynamics with fewer time points and required 
more data for comparable accuracy (Figure S11), DynNet consistently provided more accurate predictions

across all time points tested. These results underscore the robustness of DynNet in reconstructing the 
underlying dynamics of the system with limited temporal data. Beyond reconstructing the overall velocity 
field, we also evaluated DynNet's ability to capture dynamic transitions between stable states. To 
specifically validate this ability, we used a perturbed bistable system. Data was simulated from this 
system by modifying parameters to alter stable state weights without changing their positions, thereby 
capturing a clear transition from the low-X1/high-X2 state to the high-X1/low-X2 state (Figure S7 and

(Figure S12). These results support that DynNet can robustly reconstruct the global dynamics and state 
transitions of multi-stable systems. 
To validate DynNet's reliability in practical applications, we evaluated its robustness against violations of 
modeling assumptions and its optimization stability. First, regarding the robustness to noise structures, we 
verified that DynNet maintains high fidelity even when the underlying data exhibits non-diagonal 
covariance (Figure S13). Notably, explicitly modeling the full non-diagonal diffusion matrix yielded 
negligible performance gains on these simulated datasets. These results indicate that the diagonal 
diffusion assumption provides a robust approximation, effectively balancing computational efficiency 
with modeling fidelity. To further bridge the gap between continuous SDE modeling and the discrete, 
sparse nature of scRNA-seq data, we validated DynNet on Gillespie-simulated trajectories with binomial 
dropout (Figure S14). The model successfully recovered the sparse regulatory topology (with near-zero 
off-diagonal terms) and accurately quantified cell fate biases, demonstrating robustness to realistic 
measurement noise. We further evaluated the stability and efficiency of the optimization process, finding 
that the model exhibits high parametric consistency by converging to the same set of regulatory 
parameters across independent random initializations (Figure S15). Additionally, a comparison of 
initialization strategies reveals that incorporating the GRN prior significantly accelerates loss 
convergence compared to random initialization, empirically validating the computational necessity of our 
prior-guided training strategy (Figure S16).

DynNet accurately recovers simulation data for developmental branching process 
To further evaluate DynNet's performance, we constructed a gene regulatory network comprising 7 genes 
(Figure 3A and Figure S17). In this network, red arrows denote activation, while blue arrows indicate 
repression. Here, G1, G2 and G3 progressively activate G4 and G5, which mutually inhibit each other, 
forming a toggle switch, and G6 and G7 are downstream targets of G4. BoolODE, a stochastic differential 
equation-based simulator was used to simulate the multi-stable dynamics56. Training data comprise six 
temporal snapshots capturing the transition from the initial state to equilibrium, which are reduced in 
dimensionality using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Figure 3B). This system exhibits 
differentiation-like dynamics, as the system state evolves from a stem-cell-like state (I) into two distinct 
differentiation states (D1 and D2) over time. Comparing the gene expression in the two differentiation 
states, we found that genes G4, G5, G6, and G7 showed significant differences between the two stable 
states, which is consistent with the gene regulatory relationships (Figure 3C). 
DynNet successfully reconstructed the system's dynamics from simulated data. The marginal distributions 
of the estimated stable states demonstrate agreement between DynNet's estimates and the data (Figure 
3D). The estimated velocity field accurately captures gene expression trajectories toward stable states 
(Figure 3E). However, we also noted that the estimated velocity fields do not fully align with the cell 
trajectories before cell fate bifurcation. This apparent misalignment may arise because the overlap 
between pre-bifurcation states and the bifurcation points in the projection space (Figure 3B) makes it 
challenging to accurately assess the dynamics in this visualization. Despite using data generated by a 
simulator based on a different dynamic modelling framework from being used by DynNet, the model 
successfully estimated the dynamics. Using the gene expression dynamics inferred by DynNet, we 
analysed the temporal changes in individual genes. We specifically observed the cascade activation 
dynamics involving gene G1, G2, and G3 (Figure S18), which showed characteristic transient expression 
profiles with levels first increasing and then decreasing over time. Among the downstream targets, we 
focused on G6 and G7, which serve as marker genes distinguishing different cell fates. The mutual

inhibition between G4 and G5, acting as cell fate switches, then causes a significant and synchronous 
bifurcation in the expression levels of both G6 and G7, clearly marking the system's bistable fate choice 
(Figure 3F). Consistent with the MISA validation, DynNet accurately recovered the bifurcation dynamics 
and steady-state distributions from Gillespie simulation data (Figure S19), further confirming its 
robustness to realistic molecular fluctuations. We also validated the performance of DynNet on this 
dataset with an extra non-diagonal noise as environment noise (Figure S20 and S21). Despite being 
trained solely with the diagonal diffusion matrix, we confirmed the DynNet's ability to reconstruct the 
attractors with accurate noise estimation. Furthermore, quantitative accuracy showed minimal relative 
error in inferred stable state weights and agreement in the marginal density plots (Figure S22). These 
results support our modeling choice with the diagonal diffusion matrix assumption. 
We next investigated the impact of prior knowledge constraints on model inference. Using hard 
constraints where the regulatory relationships in the simulation data were predefined, the interaction type 
between two genes remains fixed during optimization, and crucially, parameters for non-existent 
interactions (not present in the prior) are rigidly masked or fixed to zero. For example, if the prior dictates 
that gene j has no inhibitory effect on gene i, the corresponding inhibitory parameter is set to zero and is 
not optimized. In contrast, soft constraints do not rigidly mask parameters but instead penalize them 
during optimization, driving them to be as small as possible (Figure S23 and S24). Figure 3H illustrates 
the training loss trajectories, and this flexibility comes at a cost: soft constraints lead to slower 
convergence compared to hard constraints due to the expanded parameter search space. Despite the 
computational cost, this difference allows soft constraints to explore the parameter space and potentially 
discover new interactions. However, we noted that when using soft constraints, the estimated parameters 
occasionally diverged from the true underlying network (Figure 3G and Figure S24). The edges not 
present in the original network yet still show nonzero estimated values after optimization (Figure 3G), 
such as the activating edge from G5 to G7. This phenomenon is biologically explicable by the indirect 
interaction in the true network: G5 inhibits G4, and G4 in turn inhibits G7. This double-inhibition induces

a positive correlation between G5 and G7 expression, which the model interprets as a direct regulatory 
edge under soft constraints. Nonetheless, for edges present in the prior network, DynNet produced 
reasonable weight predictions. While soft constraints require careful interpretation of such indirect 
relationships, they offer the significant advantage of allowing the model to infer potential regulatory 
relationships, a capability not possible with rigid hard constraints. Notably, this implies that the soft 
constraint approach may be viewed primarily as a strategy to enhance the reconstruction of system 
dynamics by capturing functional dependencies, rather than as a dedicated tool for precise structural 
network inference. Building upon this capacity to capture functional dynamics, DynNet naturally supports 
in silico perturbations. Simulated suppression of the driver gene (G4) in the 7-gene network correctly 
predicted a macroscopic shift from a developmental bifurcation to a monostable trajectory (Figure S40). 
To investigate this discovery capability when prior knowledge is incomplete, we performed experiments 
on the 7-gene network with only self-activation terms as prior, leaving all cross-gene regulatory 
interactions to be inferred. In this case, hard constraint did not lead to the desirable dynamics because of 
the loss of prior information. But DynNet successfully reconstructed the underlying dynamics with a 
possible GRN structure based on the dynamic data under soft constraint (Figure S25). Furthermore, we 
demonstrate that DynNet can learn dynamics through a two-step method of topology-hard constraint 
inference parameters in a soft constraint learning network (see Supplementary Information Section E.2 
and the Figure S36-38). To validate this structural identifiability and the utility of data-driven priors, we 
evaluated DynNet across 50 synthetic networks (see Supplementary Information Section E.4). 
Initialization with a correlation network acted as an optimization accelerator, speeding up convergence 
while enabling the soft-constraint to robustly correct false prior edges (Figure S46). Consequently, 
DynNet demonstrated reliable de novo topological recovery, maintaining robust accuracy under purely 
data-driven settings (correlation network as prior) and even when guided by a 30% corrupted prior 
(Figure S39).

Given these advantages, we further tested DynNet's inference capability under limited observational data. 
We designed a 9-dimensional gene network and considered the scenario where only a subset of genes 
could be observed and performed dynamical estimation based on this limited information (Figure S26). 
The simulated data evolved into three discrete stable states, each corresponding to distinct cell types 
characterized by the expression of genes G, H, and I. We selected a subset of genes for training DynNet to 
fit the multi-stability data generated by the model and applied soft constraints for regularization. DynNet's 
estimated stable states show alignment with the simulation data in terms of their centers, marginal 
distributions, and covariance regions. Although some deviations were observed between the estimated 
results and the data, particularly in the marginal distributions of genes E and F where two low-expression 
states were less distinctly separated in the simulation data compared to the estimates, the overall 
consistency and biological interpretability remained evident (Figure S26). The test results on the synthetic

DynNet deciphers bifurcation dynamics and fate determination in hepatocyte 
differentiation 
To assess DynNet's capability in inferring stochastic dynamics in real biological systems, we applied 
DynNet to a bifurcating dataset from hepatocyte differentiation. In vitro differentiation of pluripotent 
stem cells bifurcates into distinct cell fates, such as hepatocyte-like cells or liver bud (LB) formation, 
depending on the culture conditions49. Recent studies have systematically analyzed the differentiation 
trajectory from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to mature hepatocyte-like (MH) state and LB state. 
During early differentiation, iPSCs first transition to the definitive endoderm (DE) stage before 
progressing to the hepatic endoderm (HE) state. At this stage, distinct culture conditions drive two 
divergent pathways: conventional conditions yield immature hepatoblast-like (IH) cells that mature into 
hepatocyte-like cells, whereas organoid cultures induce the formation of LB tissues (Figure 4A). Here, we 
evaluate the feasibility of reconstructing this bifurcation process using DynNet. 
For simplicity, we identified 20 candidate key genes for further investigation through differential gene 
expression analysis and functional annotation (Figure S28). We computed the asynchronous correlation 
among candidate genes by matching data across different time points through data-driven method (see 
Supplementary Information Section F.1). This step ensures that gene regulatory relationships are captured 
while accounting for temporal dynamics. Based on the calculated asynchronous correlations, we 
estimated a gene regulatory network from the candidate genes. The estimated gene-gene interactions were 
visualized using a circular plot, with red edges indicating activation and blue edges indicating inhibition. 
This network serves as the reference framework for DynNet analysis (Figure 4B and Figure S29). 
Dimensionality reduction of the key genes revealed the underlying developmental trajectory, where the

molecular mechanisms that regulate hepatocyte differentiation. Figure 4D presents a comparison between

DynNet decodes EMT landscape switching  
To see the effectiveness of DynNet in different biological data, we applied it to a TGF-β perturbation 
lung cancer time-course dataset for EMT. We analyzed dynamics of this single-cell dataset measuring 36 
genes at seven time points (Day 0, 2, 6, 10, 12, 16, and 20) in over 100,000 cells. TGF-β was applied for 
the first 10 days to induce EMT and then withdrawn for the next 10 days to promote the mesenchymal-to-
epithelial transition (MET), a reversal in cell fate. We estimated the data-driven energy landscape from 
the real data, representing it in the projection space (Figure 5A, Figure S31-S33 and Methods) and 
performed K-means clustering to identify four clusters, corresponding to terminal states E, M, IM (MET), 
and pEMT (partial EMT state), in agreement with previous work63. First, we focused on the period from 
Day 10 to Day 20, when cell fate transitions are driven solely by gene regulations.  
The MET process begins at Day 10 and stabilizes by Day 20. To investigate these dynamics, we first 
calculated the data-driven landscape, estimated from the distribution of scRNA-seq data (Methods). This 
landscape was visualized by projecting the high-dimensional data onto a one-dimensional axis using PCA 
(Figure 5B). The position along this axis represents the progression through the MET process, and the 
height of the potential landscape at each point indicates the potential (or energy) of the corresponding cell 
state. At Day 10, the M state exhibits low energy, reflecting its stabilization following the TGF-β-induced 
transition from the E state (Figure 5B, Top). After TGF-β removal, the M state's energy increases and the 
E state's decreases (Figure 5B, Bottom). These shifts suggest that the energy landscape underlies the 
transition from the M state to the E state. The global stability of this system converges to the E state. 
To simplify DynNet's parameters and reconstruct the MET dynamics, we focused on a subset of key 
genes driving the cell fate transition. As DynNet can capture indirect regulatory interactions, reducing the 
gene to a subset should still reveal the main dynamic trajectories. We performed PCA using 𝑃𝐶𝐸 and 
𝑃𝐶𝑀, derived from E and M markers respectively, and calculated each gene's contribution along these 
axes (Figure S33). This analysis revealed that only a few genes distinguish the E and M states. Based on

Discussion  
Existing methods for inferring dynamics involve trade-offs between interpretability, data requirements, 
and scalability 18,20,21. DynNet overcomes these limitations by integrating mechanistic biophysical 
modeling with data-driven neural SDE frameworks. Unlike purely model-driven approaches constrained 
by oversimplified assumptions or black-box deep learning models that lack interpretability, DynNet 
explicitly parameterizes gene regulatory interactions using Hill functions while employing a dynamic 
noise estimator to capture the effects of stochasticity. By parameterizing causal regulatory mechanisms 
directly within the continuous drift function, it moves beyond traditional models that rely on post-hoc, 
local Jacobian matrix approximations, thereby granting the inferred dynamics robust global 
interpretability. Importantly, DynNet is fundamentally designed as a continuous dynamics modeling 
framework rather than a standalone Gene Regulatory Network (GRN) inference tool. This continuous-
time integration inherently mitigates interval-induced spurious correlations that typically plague discrete-
time models. Balancing prior biological information with data evidence, its integrated biophysical 
component captures both direct and indirect regulatory relationships within the gene network, facilitating 
dynamics inference from a minimal set of key genes directly in the original gene expression space.  
On simulation datasets (MISA and branching process), we evaluate the capability of DynNet to infer 
stable state properties, predict cell fates, and track underlying dynamics, highlighting the value of 
integrating prior knowledge via constraints. On realistic hepatocyte differentiation dataset, DynNet 
reconstructs a dynamic landscape featuring two attractor states (MH and LB) and captures bifurcation 


===== PAGE 24 =====

ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS
 
 
 
dynamics at the HE cluster, consistent with experimental observations. On EMT dataset, DynNet 
quantitatively characterizes the dynamics of EMT and MET processes, predicts transition pathways and 
identifies key genes as fate determinants, bridging molecular mechanisms with cellular decision-making. 
This capability allows for direct gene perturbation analysis and facilitates the identification of key genes 
and mechanistic rules driving cellular state changes. Ultimately, this framework presents a valuable tool 
for studying functions and mechanisms of complex biological systems, controlling cell fate transitions 
and proposing therapeutic strategies for disease28,70. 
To ensure the practical applicability and robustness of this mechanistic framework, several specific 
strategies and design choices have been incorporated into DynNet. First, to ensure computational 
feasibility in high-dimensional stochastic modeling, we adopted a diagonal diffusion matrix. Both 
theoretical and empirical validations confirm the robustness of this simplification: the continuous drift 
function effectively captures underlying gene-gene correlations, while the learned state-dependent noise 
dynamically compensates for omitted non-diagonal terms. Second, to facilitate the optimization process, 
we strategically integrated prior knowledge. While purely data-driven priors are susceptible to spurious 
correlations, our framework strictly utilizes them as optimization accelerators rather than validation 
metrics. To mitigate these inherent structural inaccuracies during de novo discovery, we employ an L2 
soft-constraint, enabling continuous data dynamics to stably override false prior edges. 
Nevertheless, there are several limitations related to structural assumptions and extreme computational 
scaling. First, while employing Hill functions significantly enhances mechanistic interpretability, it 
imposes a rigid structural assumption on the system's dynamics. This restricts the functional flexibility of 
the drift equation and constrains the optimization landscape. Second, the framework operates under the 
assumption that the biological system eventually converges to quasi-steady states, which limits its utility 
in analyzing highly oscillatory or cyclical processes, such as the cell cycle. Finally, despite our 
mathematical simplifications, the numerical integration of SDEs and the optimization of non-linear drift 
functions remain computationally demanding. Because this optimization is intensive, DynNet is 


===== PAGE 25 =====

ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS
 
 
 
inherently constrained to targeted sets of core driver genes and cannot currently process genome-wide 
transcriptomic data now. Given these boundaries, DynNet is optimally positioned as a downstream 
analytical tool for deep mechanistic dissection rather than broad de novo network discovery. 
To enhance DynNet's applicability, flexibility, and scalability, several strategies may be utilized. First, a 
gene encoding method may be adapted to analyze co-expression modules of high-dimensional gene data. 
Furthermore, a mixed modeling architecture that utilizes lightweight response functions for non-core gene 
modules can be used. To increase mechanistic modeling flexibility, it may be useful to incorporate a 
hybrid neural network architecture that combines the core mechanistic Hill function assumption with an 
assumption-free component (such as a general MLP), allowing DynNet to better describe complex system 
characteristics.

GRN Reconstruction 
Although DynNet is fundamentally designed for continuous dynamics modeling rather than standalone 
GRN inference, the constraint method provides utility of GRN reconstruction under purely data-driven 
settings. Specifically, when the model is optimized using soft constraints without any external prior 
knowledge, this procedure enables the de novo generation of a reliable reference GRN. This extracted 
topology can subsequently be utilized as a structural constraint in subsequent training phases to further 
stabilize and guide the inference of the continuous vector field. To discretize the continuous learned 
weights into a final sparse network topology without relying on arbitrary, hard-coded cutoffs, we 
implemented a data-driven thresholding strategy based on GMM (see Supplementary Information Section 
A.5). Under L2 regularization, the distribution of these normalized weights typically exhibits a dense 
component near zero (representing regularized, non-essential edges) and a long tail (representing data-
supported interactions). We fitted a two-component GMM to this distribution. The component with the 
lower mean was designated as the background noise model, parameterized by a mean (μnoise) and a

standard deviation (σnoise). The specific cutoff for retaining a structural edge was defined as the 99.7% 
confidence upper bound of this noise distribution (μnoise + 3σnoise). Edges with absolute weights 
exceeding this threshold were retained in the final discrete GRN. 
Energy landscape construction 
Understanding the stable states of a system is crucial in the study of cell fate decision. The concept of an 
energy landscape serves as a valuable tool for this, likening the system's state space to a topographical 
map where low potential energy regions correspond to more stable states, i.e., states where gene 
expression is more likely to reside. Different approaches have been developed to quantify the energy 
landscape for gene networks41,44,54. Specifically, the energy landscape can be inferred from the stable state 
probability distribution 𝑃ss(𝐱) using the formula  
𝑈(𝐱) = −ln𝑃ss(𝐱).
(15)  
We employ two distinct approaches for constructing energy landscapes depending on information 
availability: when a stochastic dynamical model (Eq. 5) is established, we quantify the landscape from 
Gaussian approximation approach; conversely, in the absence of an explicit model, we estimate the 
landscape directly from the observed data under a quasi-steady-state approximation.

Statistics and Reproducibility 
To evaluate the robustness of the DynNet framework, all computational findings were validated through 
multiple independent simulation runs using different random seeds. For the MISA and branch synthetic 
datasets, individual cells were simulated independently; the robustness of these simulations was verified 
across various random seeds, with the exact number of simulated cells detailed in respective figure 
legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Rather, sample sizes for stochastic 
simulations were chosen based on standard practices in computational systems biology to ensure the 
stability of the inferred dynamic distributions and sufficient statistical power for transition analysis. 
Specifically, for the hepatocyte dataset, FPT estimations involved 100 independent stochastic simulations 
per temporal resolution condition to properly elucidate differentiation kinetics. For the simulated gene 
knockouts, 50 independent cell trajectories were simulated for each knockout and wild-type control 
group, with statistical significance between transition delays determined using a two-sided Wilcoxon 
rank-sum test. Similarly, for the EMT dataset, the perturbation-induced changes in the minimum action 
path (𝛥𝑆) were calculated as the average over 50 independent simulation replicates. All successfully 
completed simulation runs were included in the reported results, and no data were excluded from the 
analyses. While inherent stochasticity is introduced via the simulation algorithms, the experiments were 
not randomized in terms of group allocation. Furthermore, the investigators were not blinded to allocation 
during experiments and outcome assessment, as the group assignments—such as specific in silico gene 
knockouts—were mathematically predefined by the computational models.

Datasets and prior knowledge 
This study utilizes both synthetic and experimental single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. 
Synthetic data, generated using the MISA and BoolODE models, was used to evaluate the method's 
performance56. These data were split into training and testing sets (see Supplementary Information 
Section E.1 and E.2). The MISA model simulates multi-stable gene regulatory networks, while the 
BoolODE model combines Boolean logic with ordinary differential equations. Experimental scRNA-seq 
data from publicly available sources, specifically an EMT dataset63 and a hepatocyte differentiation 
dataset49, were pre-processed using Scanpy. 
Prior knowledge about gene regulatory relationships was integrated into the model. This information, 
extracted from databases like STRING and EVEX, was used to construct a prior gene regulatory network. 
In addition, we also consider using correlation as an estimation of the gene regulatory network. Both hard 
and soft constraints are employed to guide the learning process. Hard constraints enforce consistency with 
highly reliable prior knowledge, while soft constraints allow the model to adapt when prior information is 
less certain or incomplete. These constraints help to improve the biological interpretability of the model 
by encouraging it to learn regulatory patterns consistent with existing knowledge.

Figure Captions 
Figure 1: Overview of the DynNet framework. 
A, Single-cell sequencing technology collects cells from multiple time points of a biological process and 
performs sequencing. After converting the sequencing data into a count matrix and performing 
preprocessing, the data is input into DynNet. B, By utilizing correlations and databases, a prior directed 
gene expression network can be obtained to constrain DynNet, where ⊣ and → in the network represent 
inhibitory and activating effects. C, The DynNet framework includes two sub-networks. Left is the 
Velocity Estimation Network 𝐟θ(𝐱), which extracts mechanistic features using Hill function kinetics 
(parameterized by threshold 𝑆 and coefficient 𝑛ℎ) and combines them through linear regulatory strength 
(𝑤, which are decomposed into activation matrix 𝐴 and inhibition matrix 𝐵) to predict gene expression 
velocity. Right is the Noise Estimation Network 𝐃𝜗(𝐱), which estimates the diffusion term. Both 
networks take gene expression data as input. The velocity network outputs the velocity (drift) values, 
while the noise network outputs the diffusion matrix (constrained as diagonal) representing stochastic 
fluctuations for each data point. D, A GMM is used to estimate the data characteristics of scRNA-seq, 
which serves as a prior for calculating the loss of the neural network. E, DynNet training involves 
alternating training for the velocity estimator's parameters (thresholds and regulation parameters), 
followed by independent training of the noise estimator. Key loss functions include drift loss, weight loss, 
and KL divergence. Hyperparameters for combining these losses are determined during pre-training. F, 
The dynamics modelling of DynNet is achieved by constructing and solving moment equations to obtain 
the energy landscape, which quantifies the global dynamical behaviour of the system. G, Downstream 
analysis tasks of DynNet include generating the energy landscape, analysing stable state transition paths, 
predicting bifurcation points of cell fate, and analysing potential weighted regulatory networks. H, 
DynNet’s capabilities are benchmarked against four commonly used dynamic analysis methods.
